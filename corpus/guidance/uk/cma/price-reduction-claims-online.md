# CMA compliance advice on price reduction claims (curated quotes)

_Open letter from the Competition and Markets Authority to online
businesses, published on GOV.UK on 29 March 2023 with a companion
"Examples of non-compliance". Quotes are taken from the PDF editions linked
from the source URI; footnote markers are omitted. The letter cites the
Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008, which the unfair
commercial practices provisions of the DMCC Act 2024 replaced from 6 April
2025. Curated quotes cover price reduction claims only; the urgency claim
examples are at the source URI. Curated 2026-09-30; review by kylewelsby
pending._

## The letter

### Scope

> These claims have a legitimate place when they alert consumers to genuine
> special offers or provide helpful information about current selling
> conditions. However, we have become concerned about claims that may
> mislead or put unfair pressure on consumers in the UK.

> By ‘price reduction claim’, we mean any discount or special offer price
> that refers to a higher comparison price (see Figure 2 for examples).

> The advice is intended for all businesses that are involved in selling or
> promoting the sale of anything – e.g. goods, services or digital content
> (hereafter, 'products') – online to consumers in the UK.

### What do you need to do?

> Make sure you have evidence to substantiate the urgency claims and price
> reduction claims that you make online; and keep adequate records to show
> that these claims genuinely reflect selling conditions;

> it is our view that businesses are likely to distort consumer behaviour
> and infringe the CPRs if their urgency claims or price reduction claims:
>
> - Contain information that is untrue or that is deceptive even if the
>   words used are literally true (misleading actions – regulation 5);
> - Fail to state clearly any important information the consumer needs at
>   that time to decide whether they wish to proceed (misleading omissions –
>   regulation 6);

From footnote 5:

> A claim can still be misleading even if it is factually correct. This will
> be the case where it deceives the average consumer and causes them to take
> a different decision.

## Examples of non-compliance: price reduction claims

### Example 9: comparison prices that no longer reflect the usual price

> Trader A’s claims are likely to be misleading because the time that the
> product was sold at the higher price is short compared to the time at the
> promotional price. The higher price is not now the product’s
> usual selling price, and this gives a deceptive impression of the price
> advantage the consumer enjoys by purchasing this product now.

### Example 10: comparison prices at which very few items were sold

> Trader A’s claims are likely to be misleading because the reference price
> is not a genuine and realistic selling price. The item did not sell in
> significant numbers at the higher price, and so the price reduction claim
> gives a deceptive impression of the value of the item and the saving the
> consumer could make by purchasing this item. The higher price is not the
> product’s usual selling price.

From footnote 8:

> The higher price in a price reduction claim must be a genuine and realistic
> selling price – and a business should be able to demonstrate this.

### Example 12: sales at lower prices since the comparison price was charged

> Trader A’s claims are likely to be misleading because the product has been
> offered at various prices over time. The higher price (against which the
> lower prices are being compared) is not the product’s usual selling price,
> and the price reduction claim gives a deceptive impression of the saving
> the consumer would make by purchasing the item now.
