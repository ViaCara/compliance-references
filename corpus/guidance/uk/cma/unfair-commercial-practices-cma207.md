# CMA207 - Unfair commercial practices guidance (curated quotes)

_Guidance on the unfair commercial practices provisions in Chapter 1 of Part 4
of, and Schedule 20 to, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act
2024. Published by the Competition and Markets Authority on 4 April 2025 and
updated on 18 November 2025. The provisions apply to commercial practices
from 6 April 2025. Quotes are taken from the HTML edition; footnote markers
are omitted. Curated quotes only; the full guidance is at the source URI.
Curated 2026-09-28; review by kylewelsby pending._

## Chapter 1 - Overview

### Overview of the prohibitions contained in the UCP provisions

> Schedule 20 of the DMCC Act covers 32 specified practices which are in
> all circumstances considered unfair. Go to chapter 3 for more detail.
>
> Section 226 covers the following prohibited conduct:
> - provision of false or misleading information
> - deceptive overall presentation
> - creating confusion with competitors’ products
> - failing to comply with requirements in a code of conduct

### Practices that are always unfair

> There are 32 commercial practices listed in the DMCC Act which are
> unfair in all circumstances. These include fake reviews, misuse of trust
> marks, pressure selling and sales of illegal products. These are covered
> in chapter 3.
>
> If a trader engages in any of these practices they are acting
> unlawfully.

## Chapter 3 - Banned practices

### Summary at a glance

> There are 32 commercial practices which are unfair in all circumstances.
>
> There is no need to consider the likely effect of these practices on
> consumers to prove an infringement of the law.
>
> Schedule 20 to the DMCC Act lists 32 banned commercial practices.
> Breaches of these provisions may also breach the other prohibitions in
> the UCP provisions.

### Banned practice 3

> Displaying a trust mark, quality mark or equivalent without having
> obtained the necessary authorisation.

Example:

> A trader puts a kitemark logo, which certifies the safety and quality of
> an item, on a safety helmet they are selling. In fact, their product
> does not qualify for it as the safety helmet has not undergone the
> necessary repeat testing to renew its certification.

### Banned practice 4

> Claiming that a trader, a trader’s commercial practice, or a product has
> been approved, endorsed or authorised by a public or private body when
> either:
> - the claim is false
> - the terms of the approval, endorsement or authorisation have not been,
>   or are not being, complied with

Example:

> A plumber claims that they are registered and approved by a well-known
> trader recommendation platform when they are not.

## Chapter 5 - Misleading actions

### Summary at a glance

> 4 types of misleading action are prohibited if the average consumer is
> likely to take a different decision as a result:
> - providing false or misleading information
> - an overall presentation that is likely to deceive the average consumer
> - marketing which creates or is likely to create confusion with another
>   trader, or their product or distinguishing mark
> - failing to comply with a requirement in a code of conduct which a
>   trader purports to be complying with

### Misleading action type 1: False or misleading information

From the list of types of information which may impact on a consumer’s
decision if it is false:

> - any statement or symbol relating to direct or indirect sponsorship or
>   approval of the trader or the product
> - the nature, attributes and rights of the trader or their agent,
>   including their identity, assets, qualifications, status, approval,
>   affiliations or connections, awards and distinctions, ownership of
>   industrial, commercial or intellectual property rights etc

### Misleading action type 3: Creating confusion with competitors’ products

> Commercial practices are also prohibited as misleading actions if:
> - they market a product in a way which creates, or is likely to create,
>   confusion with any products, trade marks, trade names or other
>   distinguishing marks of another trader
> - this is likely to cause the average consumer to take a different
>   decision as a result

Examples:

> A major sunglasses retailer is selling ‘Sunspex’, a highly successful
> brand with a signature pink colour. A competing trader launches
> ‘Sunspeks’ in a slightly different shade of pink. This will be illegal
> if the average consumer is likely to be confused and more likely to take
> a different decision, such as to buy Sunspeks, than they otherwise would
> be.

> A trader pays for search results to be shown to consumers who search for
> a competitor’s product. The listing leads to a website with a domain
> name that suggests it is operated by that competitor when in fact it is
> not. As a result, consumers purchase products from that website,
> believing it to be the competitor’s site.
