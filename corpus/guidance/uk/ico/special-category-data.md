## What are the 'special categories of personal data'?

> The UK GDPR singles out some types of personal data as likely to be more sensitive. It gives extra protection to:
>
> * personal data revealing racial or ethnic origin;
> * personal data revealing political opinions;
> * personal data revealing religious or philosophical beliefs;
> * personal data revealing trade union membership;
> * genetic data;
> * biometric data (where used for identification purposes);
> * data concerning health;
> * data concerning a person's sex life; and
> * data concerning a person's sexual orientation.
>
> The UK GDPR doesn't define most of the special categories, which are fairly self-explanatory. However, it gives specific definitions for genetic data, biometric data and health data.

## Why is this data special?

> It's not just that these types of information might be seen as more sensitive or private. These types of data merit special protection because their use can create significant risks to people's fundamental rights and freedoms. For example, the various categories are closely linked with:
>
> * freedom of thought, conscience and religion;
> * freedom of expression;
> * freedom of assembly and association;
> * the right to bodily integrity;
> * the right to respect for private and family life; or
> * freedom from discrimination.
>
> These types of information require greater care because collecting and using them is more likely to interfere with these fundamental rights or put someone more at risk of discrimination. This reflects the UK GDPR's risk-based approach.
>
> The legislation frames the special categories of personal data broadly. This means they can include information that may not feel particularly sensitive. For example, details about a person's mental health can be more sensitive than information about a broken leg. However, both are data concerning health. Because of the potential risks to people's fundamental rights, it is important that you identify any special category data and handle it carefully. This applies even if you don't think the information is particularly sensitive.

## What is health data?

> The UK GDPR defines health data in article 4(1)(15):
>
> "'data concerning health' means personal data related to the physical or mental health of a natural person, including the provision of health care services, which reveal information about his or her health status".
>
> Health data can be about a person's past, current or future health status. It covers specific details of medical conditions, tests or treatment as well as any related data which reveals anything about the state of someone's health.
>
> Health data can therefore include a wide range of personal data, such as:
>
> * any information on injury, disease, disability or disease risk;
> * medical history, medical opinions, diagnosis and clinical treatment;
> * medical examination data and test results;
> * information collected from a person when they register for health services or access treatment; and
> * a number, symbol or other identifier assigned to a person to uniquely identify them for health purposes (eg an NHS number, a Community Health Index number in Scotland or a Health and Care Number in Northern Ireland), if combined with information revealing something about the state of their health.
>
> It's not always obvious when something might be health data. For example, 'the provision of health care services' part of the definition covers things that tell you about a person's health, such as:
>
> * appointment details;
> * reminders; and
> * invoices.
>
> The key point is that the information needs to reveal something about a person's health status. A GP appointment in isolation doesn't tell you anything about a person's health, as this might be a simple check-up or screening appointment. However, you might reasonably infer a person's health status from their list of appointments at an osteopath clinic or from an invoice for a series of physiotherapy sessions. These things can tell you what sort of treatment the person may be receiving. This would then count as health data.
>
> Information from body-worn devices, such as fitness trackers, isn't necessarily health data. This is because the information, such as step counts, doesn't itself reveal anything about a person's health status. For example, a person's fitness tracker may record a low number of step counts for several reasons, such as lack of use or battery failure.
>
> This information is only health data if it reveals something about a person's health status. This might be if a heart rate is recorded, or if a device records BMI scores. In these cases, you can infer someone's health status from this information.

## What about inferences?

> The UK GDPR is clear that special category data includes not only personal data that specifies relevant details, but also personal data revealing or concerning these details.
>
> If the information itself clearly reveals or concerns something specific and definite about one of the special categories, that counts as special category data. For example, a statement that says Mr X is married to Mr Y clearly reveals information about Mr X's and Mr Y's sexual orientation.
>
> If the information itself doesn't clearly reveal or concern something about one of the special categories, it may still be possible to infer or guess details about someone that do fall within those categories.
>
> For example, you may be able to infer a person's religion or ethnicity from their name or images of them. This is because many surnames or modes of dress can be associated with a particular ethnicity or religion.
>
> But you don’t have to treat all such names or images as special category data in every instance. For example, you don’t need a special category condition just to hold these names or images on a customer database.
>
> Whether or not inferred data counts as special category data and triggers article 9 depends on whether:
>
> * your processing intends to make an inference linked to one of the special categories of data; or
> * you intend to treat someone differently on the basis of inferred information linked to one of the special categories of data.
>
> Where either of these apply, the inferred data is special category data regardless of how confident you are that the inference is correct.
>
> If you carry out any form of profiling which infers things like ethnicity, beliefs, politics, health status (condition or risks), sexual orientation or sex life, you will be processing special category data and must identify an article 9 condition for processing.
>
> If you're using inferred data to influence how you deal with people, you still have a duty under the accuracy and data minimisation principles to ensure you're not using personal data that is:
>
> * inaccurate;
> * inadequate; or
> * irrelevant for the purposes for which it's being processed.
>
> Even if you're not drawing an inference or treating people differently, there may still be an obvious risk that other people might (rightly or wrongly) infer something from the data that might be considered sensitive or private. If this is the case (especially if this relates to the special categories of data), you should think carefully about how you ensure fairness and whether you can do anything more to minimise those privacy risks.

## What does the UK GDPR say?

> Article 9 prohibits the processing of special category data. There are 10 exceptions to this general prohibition, often referred to as 'conditions for processing special category data':
>
> (a) Explicit consent(b) Employment, social security and social protection (if authorised by law)(c) Vital interests(d) Not-for-profit bodies(e) Made public by the data subject(f) Legal claims or judicial acts(g) Reasons of substantial public interest (with a basis in law)(h) Health or social care (with a basis in law or based on a contract with a health professional)(i) Public health (with a basis in law)(j) Archiving, research and statistics (with a basis in law)
>
> Five of the conditions only apply if your processing has an authorisation or basis in law. The Data Protection Act (DPA) sets this out and it's covered below. (See What does the DPA say?)
>
> The UK GDPR also restricts you from using special category data to make solely automated decisions that have legal or similarly significant effects on a person.
>
> Article 22B says you can only do this where one of the following conditions applies:
>
> * You base the decision entirely on the person's explicit consent.
> * The decision is necessary for a contract between the person and an organisation and a substantial public interest condition applies.
> * The decision is required or authorised by law and there is a substantial public interest condition.
>
> And as with all solely automated significant decisions, you must implement safeguards to protect people's rights, freedoms and interests.
>
> Use of special category data, particularly on a large scale, can also affect your other obligations, such as the need for:
>
> * documentation;
> * DPIAs;
> * DPOs; and
> * UK representatives.

## What does the DPA say?

> The DPA supplements and tailors the UK GDPR conditions for processing special category data.
>
> Section 10 of the DPA says that if you're relying on a UK GDPR condition that requires authorisation by law or a basis in UK law, you must meet one of the additional conditions in schedule 1.
>
> The first part of schedule 1 provides a specific basis in UK law for relying on certain article 9 conditions:
>
> * condition 1: employment, social security and social protection – article 9(2)(b);
> * condition 2: health or social care – article 9(2)(h);
> * condition 3: public health – article 9(2)(i); and
> * condition 4: archiving, research or statistics – article 9(2)(j).
>
> The second part of schedule 1 lays out a further 23 'substantial public interest' conditions for the purposes of article 9(2)(g). (See What are the substantial public interest conditions?)
>
> For these 23 conditions and the condition for employment, social security and social protection, you must:
>
> * have an appropriate policy document (unless an exemption applies); and
> * include information about the condition you rely on in your record of processing activities.
>
> Section 11(1) applies to the health or social care condition and clarifies when the requirement for a professional obligation of secrecy will be met under UK law. If you're relying on the health or social care condition, you must consider the relevant parts of schedule 3 of the DPA.
>
> The DPA doesn't add any more specific conditions for genetic data biometric data or health data.

## What is the combined effect of these rules?

> You must ensure your processing is lawful, fair and transparent and complies with all other principles and requirements of the UK GDPR.
>
> Remember that for your processing to be lawful, you must always identify an article 6 lawful basis for processing.
>
> In addition, to process special category data you must meet one of the conditions in article 9 of the UK GDPR, together with any associated DPA schedule conditions where required.
>
> You must be able to demonstrate that your processing meets the specific requirements of the relevant conditions. (For more detail on each condition, see What are the conditions for processing?)

## How do the conditions work?

> First, you must be clear about why you need special category data, as most of the conditions are based on the specific purpose for the processing. You can then identify the most relevant condition.
>
> Given the potential risks to people's rights, the conditions are narrowly drawn and often require you to meet detailed criteria and put in place specific safeguards and accountability measures.
>
> Some conditions are also limited to specific types of controllers and some only apply to particular types of special category data.
>
> If you're not sure which condition is appropriate, it can be useful to start by considering whether you can reasonably give people a choice and get explicit consent for your processing. However, explicit consent isn't always appropriate. For example, it may not always be appropriate for the public sector. If there are good reasons why consent won't work, you can then consider the other article 9 conditions.
>
> You must focus on your purpose for processing, ensuring that the special category data is necessary for that purpose. If the only relevant condition is substantial public interest, you must go on to consider the specific substantial public interest conditions in the DPA.
>
> If none of the conditions cover your purpose and you can't obtain valid explicit consent, you can't process the special category data.
>
> The only potential exemption from article 9 is the public interest exemption for journalism, academia, art or literature.
>
> There are no other exemptions from article 9.

## What does 'necessary' mean?

> Most of the conditions depend on you being able to demonstrate that the processing is 'necessary' for a specific purpose. This doesn't mean the processing has to be absolutely essential. But it needs to be more than just useful or habitual. It has to be a targeted and proportionate way of achieving that purpose.
>
> If you can reasonably achieve the same purpose by less intrusive means (eg without using special category data), the processing isn't 'necessary'. This links to the data minimisation principle, which you must comply with for all the processing you do, including special category data.
>
> It isn't enough to argue that processing is necessary because it's standard practice or part of your business model, processes or procedures. The question is whether the processing of the special category data is a targeted and proportionate way of achieving the purpose described in the condition.

## Do the conditions affect our lawful basis?

> No. The special category conditions don't replace or override the fact that you must have a lawful basis for processing. Instead, the conditions operate as an additional layer of protection on top of the normal rules.
>
> If you're processing special category data, you must still identify a lawful basis for your processing in exactly the same way as for any other personal data. In other words, you must identify both:
>
> * a lawful basis under article 6; and
> * a condition for processing special category data under article 9.
>
> Your choice of lawful basis under article 6 doesn't dictate which condition you have to apply and vice versa. You can choose whichever condition best fits the circumstances, no matter your lawful basis.
>
> In some cases, there may be an obvious link between the lawful basis and a particular condition. For example, if your lawful basis is vital interests, the special category condition for vital interests may also be appropriate. And if your lawful basis is consent, it likely makes sense to use explicit consent for special category data.
>
> However, some of the lawful bases don't have a direct link with a particular special category condition (for example, contract or legitimate interests). This is because the conditions for special category data are intentionally more restrictive and specific. This doesn't mean that you will never have a condition. But you may need to look at all of them to identify one that fits the circumstances and justifies that element of your processing.
>
> In particular, even if you're not using consent as your lawful basis for all the data, you could still consider explicit consent as your condition for processing any special category data.
>
> If you want to rely on legitimate interests as your lawful basis, the sensitive nature of special category data means there are greater risks to people's interests, rights and freedoms. Therefore, to rely on this lawful basis, you should consider if you:
>
> * need a more compelling justification for what you want to do to balance these risks; and
> * can put in place more robust safeguards to mitigate any impact or risks to people to demonstrate that the legitimate interests basis applies.

## Do we need to do a DPIA?

> You must do a DPIA before beginning any type of processing that's likely to be high risk. This means you're more likely to need to do a DPIA for special category data and be aware of the likely risks.
>
> In particular, you must carry out a DPIA if you plan to process special category data:
>
> * on a large scale;
> * to determine access to a product, service, opportunity or benefit; or
> * which includes genetic data or biometric data for the purpose of uniquely identifying someone.
>
> If in doubt, you should carry out a DPIA. This will make it easier to ensure you have appropriate safeguards in place and can demonstrate your compliance.

## What else do we need to do?

> You must always ensure that your processing is lawful, fair and transparent and complies with all the other principles and requirements of the UK GDPR.
>
> You should be aware that the particular risks associated with special category data might affect what is fair or what you need to do to comply.
>
> In particular, you must consider the following:
>
> * Data minimisation: you must collect and retain only the minimum amount of special category data and justify why you need this specific type of data.
> * Security measures: one of the considerations for determining the appropriate level of security is the sensitivity of the personal data. Consider whether you need additional security measures for special category data.
> * Transparency: you must include information about categories of data in your privacy notice and other privacy information for people. If you're processing special category data, you must make this clear and specify which categories of data they are. You don't have to say which condition you're relying on, but you must state your lawful basis as you would for any other personal data.
> * Documentation: if you process special category data you must keep records, including documenting the categories of data. You must also identify whether you need an appropriate policy document under the DPA. If so, your record of processing must include:
> * your condition for processing the data;
> * how you satisfy a lawful basis for that processing; and
> * specific details about whether you have followed your retention and deletion policies (and if not, why not).
>
> If you intend to process special category data in particular ways, there are also other considerations:
>
> * DPO: if your core activities (in other words, your primary business objectives) require large-scale processing of special category data, you must appoint a DPO.
> * UK representative: if you're not established in the UK but you offer services to or monitor people in the UK, and you process special category data on a large scale, you must designate a representative in the UK. You must appoint a representative even for occasional small-scale processing of special category data, unless you can show that it's low risk.

## (a) Explicit consent

> Article 9(2)(a) permits you to process special category data if:
>
> "the data subject has given explicit consent to the processing of those personal data for one or more specified purposes".
>
> The UK GDPR doesn't define 'explicit consent', though it's likely to be similar to the usual high standard of consent as a lawful basis. In particular, it must be freely given, specific, affirmative (opt-in) and unambiguous, and able to be withdrawn at any time. In practice, the extra requirements for consent to be 'explicit' include:
>
> * explicit consent must be confirmed by the person in a clear statement (whether oral or written), rather than by any other type of affirmative action; and
> * it must specify the nature of the special category data.
>
> In general, you should ensure explicit consent is separate from any other consents you're seeking.
>
> Explicit consent is the only condition that can apply to a wide range of circumstances. In some cases, it may be your only option. If so, you must make sure that you offer people genuine choice over whether you use their information and how you use it.
>
> You should be particularly careful if you ask for consent as a condition of your services, or you're in a position of power over the person (for example, if you're a public authority or their employer).
>
> If you need to process special category data to provide a contractual service to a person, explicit consent may be available as your condition for processing that data, even if it's a condition of service. But you must demonstrate consent is still freely given. In particular, that the processing is objectively necessary to perform the contractual service and not just included in your terms for broader business purposes.

## (h) Health or social care

> Article 9(2)(h) permits you to process special category data if:
>
> "processing is necessary for the purposes of preventive or occupational medicine, for the assessment of the working capacity of the employee, medical diagnosis, the provision of health or social care or treatment or the management of health or social care systems and services on the basis of domestic law or pursuant to contract with a health professional and subject to the conditions and safeguards referred to in paragraph 3".
>
> The DPA sets out the relevant basis in UK law in schedule 1 condition 2. This condition covers the following purposes:
>
> * preventive or occupational medicine;
> * the assessment of an employee's working capacity;
> * medical diagnosis;
> * the provision of health care or treatment;
> * the provision of social care, which likely includes social work, personal care and social support services;
> * the management of health care systems or services; or
> * the management of social care systems or services.
>
> You must be able to justify why processing of this specific data is necessary. It has to be a reasonable and proportionate way of achieving one of these purposes. You must not have more data than you need.
>
> Article 9(3) of the UK GDPR contains an additional safeguard. To rely on this condition, you must ensure the personal data is being processed by (or under the responsibility of) a professional who is subject to an obligation of professional secrecy. Section 11 of the DPA makes it clear that, in the UK, this includes:
>
> * a health professional or a social work professional; or
> * another person who in the circumstances owes a duty of confidentiality under an enactment or rule of law.
>
> Section 204 of the DPA defines the terms 'health professional' and 'social work professional'. As a guide these terms include:
>
> * doctors;
> * nurses or midwives;
> * dentists;
> * opticians or optometrists;
> * osteopaths;
> * chiropractors;
> * arts therapists;
> * chiropodists;
> * clinical scientists;
> * dietitians;
> * medical laboratory technicians;
> * occupational therapists;
> * orthoptists;
> * paramedics;
> * physiotherapists;
> * prosthetists or orthotists;
> * radiographers;
> * speech and language therapists;
> * pharmacists or pharmacy technicians;
> * child psychotherapists; and
> * social workers.
>
> A duty of confidence exists when someone has given their personal information with the understanding that it will be kept confidential. Any care home staff who are directly involved in providing care to a resident will have a legitimate relationship with that person. It's therefore acceptable for them to view confidential information to support the provision of care.
>
> If you're not subject to a duty of confidentiality to the person, but you're under a legal obligation in connection with the provision of social services, the condition for employment, social security and social protection law may be more appropriate. (See (b) Employment, social security and social protection law.)
>
> You don't need an appropriate policy document in place for this condition.
>
> Where this condition applies, the person doesn't have a right to erasure.

## (j) Archiving, research and statistics

> Article 9(2)(j) permits you to process special category data if:
>
> "processing is necessary for archiving purposes in the public interest, scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes, is carried out in accordance with Article 84B and is based on domestic law which shall be proportionate to the aim pursued and provide for suitable and specific measures to safeguard the fundamental rights and the interests of the data subject".
>
> The DPA sets out the relevant basis in UK law in schedule 1 condition 4. This condition requires you to:
>
> * demonstrate that the processing is necessary for archiving, research or statistical purposes – it must be a reasonable and proportionate way of achieving one of these purposes and you must not have more data than you need;
> * comply with the safeguards and restrictions set out in articles 84B and 84C of the UK GDPR; and
> * demonstrate that the processing is in the public interest. The UK GDPR doesn't define the term 'public interest' but you need to point to a benefit to the wider public or society as a whole. It's not enough to refer to your own interests or the interests of the particular person.
>
> This condition doesn't cover all research. You must demonstrate that your research is either scientific or historical in nature and in the public interest.
>
> This applies to both public sector and private sector research. It can include, for example, technological development and demonstration, fundamental research and applied research and privately funded research.
>
> It may, therefore, cover commercial scientific research, but you need to demonstrate that the research uses rigorous scientific methods and furthers a general public interest.
>
> Article 84B(2) says that you must have appropriate safeguards in place to protect people. Article 84C explains what safeguards you must put in place for this requirement to be satisfied. You must:
>
> * demonstrate that the processing isn't likely to cause substantial damage or distress to the people whose information you're processing;
> * not use the data to take any action or make decisions about the people concerned, unless you're carrying out approved medical research as defined in article 84C(5); and
> * put in place safeguards that include technological and organisational measures to ensure data minimisation, such as pseudonymisation.
>
> You don't need an appropriate policy document in place.

Disclosure to elected representatives25. Informing elected representatives about prisoners26. Publication of legal judgments27. Anti-doping in sport28. Standards of behaviour in sport
>
> The substantial public interest conditions give you the basis in law for relying on article 9(2)(g). They allow you to use special category data for a variety of specific purposes. For example, if you need to use special category data to carry out a legal function or to monitor and promote equality and diversity at your organisation.
>
> Having clear reasons for using the information helps you identify the most relevant condition(s). You should consider the detail of that condition carefully, and you must be able to show that it applies. This is because many of the conditions list several specific criteria that you must meet.
>
> Nothing in these conditions exempts you from assessing whether your processing is necessary and proportionate in the substantial public interest. You must still do this under article 9(2)(g).
>

For other conditions, the substantial public interest element is built in. This assumes that processing under that condition is in the substantial public interest (eg preventing fraud, administration of justice and parliamentary purposes). However, even where this is the case, depending on the specific condition, you may have to meet additional obligations. For example, equality of opportunity or treatment will only apply where the processing isn't likely to cause a person substantial damage or substantial distress. ## What are 'reasons of substantial public interest'?

> The DPA and the UK GDPR don't define the term 'substantial public interest'. The public interest covers a wide range of values and principles about the public good or what's in the best interests of society.
>
> Commercial or private interests aren't the same as a public interest. Therefore, if you need to rely on reasons of substantial public interest, it isn't enough to point to your own interests. You can have a private interest but you need to be able to point to a wider public benefit.
>
> Substantial public interest means the public interest needs to be real and of substance. As using special category data carries particular risks, it's not enough to make a vague or generic public interest argument. You should be able to make specific arguments about the concrete wider benefits of your processing. For example, you could think about how your processing benefits the public in terms of both depth (ie the amount of benefit experienced from the processing, even if only by a few people) and breadth (ie the number of people benefitting from the processing).
>
> You should focus on showing that your overall purpose for processing special category data has substantial public interest benefits. If your overall purpose is of substantial public interest, you don't need to make separate public interest arguments or show specific benefits each time you use the information or for each item of special category data. But you must be able to show that all your processing under the relevant condition is necessary for that purpose and complies with the data minimisation principle.

## What is the role of explicit consent?

> For many of the substantial public interest conditions, you must first consider whether you can give people a choice and rely on their explicit consent to process their special category data. But there may be a good reason why you can't ask people for explicit consent. For example, you might not want to ask for explicit consent if you're investigating someone and doing so might prejudice your investigation. Alternatively, there may be circumstances where it's difficult for you to get valid explicit consent. For example, public authorities, employers and other organisations in a position of power may not be able to show that explicit consent has been freely given.
>
> If you have a reason for not getting explicit consent or if you think it wouldn't be valid, you should check the detail of the relevant condition to see exactly what justification you need.

## What is an appropriate policy document?

> An appropriate policy document is a short document outlining your compliance measures and retention policies for special category data (or criminal offence data if you're processing that type of data). The DPA says you must have an appropriate policy document in place for almost all the substantial public interest conditions and the employment, social security and social protection condition.
>
> (For more information on the last condition, see (b) Employment, social security and social protection law.)
>
> This document doesn't have to take any particular form, as long as it briefly outlines:
>
> * the substantial public interest condition(s) you're relying on;
> * your procedures for complying with each of the data protection principles;
> * your retention and deletion policies; and
> * an indication of how long you're planning to keep the specific information.
>
> If you process special category data for several different purposes, you don't need a separate policy document for each condition or processing activity. You could use one document to cover them all. But you must provide people with enough information to understand how you're using their special category data and how long you will keep it for.
>
> If you have carried out a DPIA for your intended processing, you could reuse part of this (particularly the section on necessity and proportionality) to help you draft your appropriate policy document.
>
> You must keep your appropriate policy document until six months after the date you stop the relevant processing. You must keep it under review and update it when necessary. You should publish it as a matter of good practice. If we ask to see it, you must provide it to us free of charge.
>
> You must also include the following details in your general documentation:
>
> * how your processing satisfies a lawful basis;
> * your condition for processing special category data; and
> * whether you have followed your retention and deletion policies (and if not, why not).
