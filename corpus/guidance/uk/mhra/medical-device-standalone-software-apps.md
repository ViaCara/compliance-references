# Medical device stand-alone software including apps (MHRA, curated quotes)

_MHRA guidance on when stand-alone software and apps placed on the Great
Britain market qualify as medical devices under UK MDR 2002. Version
v1.10f, last updated 1 July 2023. Curated quotes only; the full guidance is
the PDF linked from the source URI. Reviewed 2026-10-20 by kylewelsby._

## Intended purpose and disclaimers

> Care should be taken with the description of what the software is
> intended to be used for. Simple changes to the description make the
> difference between a product being considered a device or not.

> General disclaimers (for example ‘this product is not a medical device’)
> are not acceptable if medical claims are made or implied elsewhere in the
> product labelling or associated promotional literature.

> A manufacturer’s stated view of their product is not solely determinative
> as to whether their device is or is not a medical device. Based on the
> surrounding circumstances e.g. the labelling, instructions for use,
> promotional material, its mode of action and manner of use as perceived
> by the consumer, it is possible for an objective observer such as the
> MHRA or an averagely informed consumer to view a product as a medical
> device.

## Decision support

Software is unlikely to be a device if:

> It has decision points, options may be explained but the health care
> professional decides which path to take.

> It offers only lifestyle treatment choices or referral advice (e.g. see
> your GP).

Software is most likely to be a device if:

> It is intended to influence the actual treatment - dose, size of
> implant, time of treatment etc.

## Diagnosis

Examples that may be devices include:

> Apps and software that provide medical condition advice based on user
> entered data.

## Symptom checkers

Examples that may be devices include:

> Software that provides treatment recommendations for listed conditions,
> e.g. first aid treatment.

Examples that are unlikely to be devices include:

> Software that offers only reference information about the conditions
> listed.

> Software that only signposts the user to suitable care e.g. see your GP,
> go to A&E.

## Appendix 1 – Symptom checkers

Appendix 1 describes independent software for lay users that matches
entered symptoms with conditions. Its outputs can include:

> An indication of seriousness – e.g. ‘Red flag’.

> Triage 'signposting' of next steps, e.g. visit GP, go to A&E.

Examples that may be devices include:

> Software that indicates the likelihood of a match.

> Software that offers filters by red flag/severity/probability of a match.

Examples that are unlikely to be devices include:

> Software that only signposts the user to suitable care e.g. see your GP,
> go to A&E.

On classification:

> Symptom checker devices will be class I unless considered to ‘allow
> direct diagnosis’, in which case they will be class IIa.

> For devices intended to be used by lay users, provision of an indicative
> diagnosis may be enough to imply that the device is allowing direct
> diagnosis.
